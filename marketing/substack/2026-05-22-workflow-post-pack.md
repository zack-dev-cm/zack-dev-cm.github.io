## Draft

Most technical posts fail in a boring way.

They announce that something shipped. They mention the stack. They add a screenshot. Then they end with a link.

That is fine as a status update, but it is weak as a Substack post.

A good Substack post should do one more job: it should leave behind a workflow.

The workflow can be small. A checklist. A test plan. A prompt. A release gate. A source ledger. A table that helps a reader make a decision. The point is not to turn every essay into documentation. The point is to give the reader a concrete artifact they can use after the story is over.

That rule is becoming my filter for AI Agent Field Notes.

If I write about a Chrome extension launch, the reader should leave with a public-surface checklist. If I write about a model release, the reader should leave with migration tests. If I write about a generated image or video, the reader should leave with a proof gate that separates artifact value from fake evidence.

The post is the narrative. The workflow is the receipt.

## Why This Matters Now

Substack is not just an email tool. It is also an archive, a search surface, a feed, a publication page, and a trust layer around a writer's repeated work.

Substack's own SEO guidance says the author's website and links into the publication matter for discoverability. Its support docs also describe native signup embeds, post embeds, and RSS/import flows. That means the publication can connect to a portfolio, but the connection only helps if the posts themselves earn trust.

For a builder portfolio, trust does not come from vague thought leadership.

It comes from public proof:

- a shipped project page
- a dated release note
- a public repo
- a live store listing
- a benchmark or validation command
- a screenshot that does not leak private data
- a post that says exactly what the evidence does and does not prove

That is why the workflow matters. A workflow turns the post from "look what I did" into "here is the reusable move behind what I did."

## The Structure I Want Every Serious Post To Pass

Here is the minimum shape I want for portfolio-backed Substack posts.

First, name the reader problem in the opening paragraph.

Not the technology. Not the product name. The actual pain.

"My portfolio stats drifted from the source of truth" is stronger than "I updated my portfolio."

"A model release note is not a migration plan" is stronger than "DeepSeek released a new model."

"Generated product ads are useful as proof artifacts, but they are not launch-ready by default" is stronger than "AI video is changing marketing."

Second, show the artifact.

This can be a screenshot, repo file, generated markdown page, release checklist, public listing, or validation output. The artifact gives the post weight. It also prevents the post from floating into generic advice.

Third, explain the decision.

Readers do not need every implementation detail. They need the reasoning that transfers. Why did I choose a manual signup fallback before connecting Substack embeds? Why did I keep generated images as illustration and not evidence? Why did I treat live publishing as an approval-gated action?

Fourth, name the limit.

This is the part most posts skip.

Public downloads do not prove active users. A generated illustration does not prove a product works. A browser screenshot from a logged-in account is not automatically safe to publish. A Substack RSS feed proves the post exists, but not that readers found it valuable.

Naming the limit makes the proof stronger.

Fifth, leave the workflow.

The reader should finish with something like this:

1. Pick one concrete reader problem.
2. Attach one public artifact.
3. Add one source ledger.
4. Name one thing the evidence does not prove.
5. End with one reusable checklist, command, prompt, or skill contract.
6. Use one CTA.

That is the whole shape.

## Where AI Helps And Where It Does Not

AI is useful in this loop, but not as a substitute for taste.

I want AI to help with outlines, source checks, typo cleanup, title variants, image prompts, and leak scans. I also want AI-generated illustrations when a post needs a strong visual metaphor.

But I do not want AI to invent evidence.

If an image shows a fake dashboard, it is decoration. If a post claims a launch worked, it needs dated public evidence. If a post says a workflow is reusable, it needs a command, checklist, or skill that can be inspected.

The generated hero image for this post is useful because it shows the idea: portfolio, RSS, draft, source cards, publish checklist. It is not useful as proof. The proof is the actual workflow and the public links.

That distinction is small, but it protects the whole publication.

## What This Does Not Prove

This does not prove that Substack will grow by itself.

It does not prove that every post needs a technical artifact.

It does not prove that a generated illustration improves conversion.

It does not prove that a reader wants every implementation detail.

The only claim is narrower: a technical Substack post gets stronger when it leaves behind a reusable workflow and connects its claims to public proof.

That is the rule I want to test.

## The Reusable Checklist

Before publishing a portfolio-backed Substack post, I want the draft to pass this gate:

- The first paragraph names the reader pain.
- The post includes one public artifact.
- The title is literal enough to search and share.
- Every current claim has a public source.
- The post says what the proof does not prove.
- Generated images are disclosed as generated.
- The CTA is one action, not five.
- The final draft has no local paths, credentials, private analytics, or subscriber data.
- The portfolio is updated only after the public Substack URL exists.

If a post cannot pass that gate, it is not ready. It may still be a useful note to self, but it is not a public field note yet.
